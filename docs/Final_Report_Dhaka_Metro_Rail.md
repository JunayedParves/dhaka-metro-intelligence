# Dhaka Metro Rail: Public Investment Performance, Economic Returns and the Future of Urban Mobility in Bangladesh

Independent assessment of MRT Line-6. Research cut-off: 13 November 2026.

## How to read this report

Three kinds of numbers appear here, and they carry different weight.

- **Reported** figures come from government, JICA or audited documents, or from the press where marked. They are facts as published.
- **Simulated** figures come from the synthetic operating datasets built for this study, because DMTCL publishes no daily, station-level or cost data. They are calibrated to the published totals but are not observations.
- **Modelled** figures (time savings, emissions, economic returns) rest on assumptions listed in the assumptions register. They indicate order of magnitude only.

The assessment used public sources only. No data was requested from DMTCL, DTCA, IMED or the Planning Commission. "Dhaka City Corporation" does not exist as a single body; the city is run by Dhaka North and Dhaka South City Corporations, and neither has a role in the project.

## Summary

MRT Line-6 has succeeded as a transport service and under-delivered as an investment case. It carries about 450,000–500,000 passengers a day, at its design level, with strong user satisfaction. It cost 49% more than approved, and the project will close four and a half years late. Ticket revenue appears to cover day-to-day operating cost but not loan repayments, which reach roughly Tk 1,000 crore a year by 2030. On the direct benefits to passengers that can be estimated from public data, the economic return is well below the 12% threshold and far below the 25–28% in JICA's appraisals. The gap comes mainly from shorter trips than planned, the higher cost, and benefits to road users that cannot be verified. The modelled return is low-confidence and would improve as connecting lines open, but the evidence does not support approving further lines on the appraisal assumptions used for Line-6.

## 1. How much has Bangladesh invested?

The approved cost is **Tk 32,717.73 crore**: Tk 12,521.97 crore from the government and Tk 20,195.76 crore in JICA loans (reported, RTHD). By 30 September 2026, **Tk 27,029 crore (82.6%)** had been spent. JICA committed 255,798 million yen across five loans on concessional terms: 30 years, 10 years' grace, interest around 0.7%.

## 2. How did the budget change?

| Stage | Approved | Cost (Tk crore) | Government share | End date |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Original | December 2012 | 21,985 | 24.5% | June 2024 |
| Second revision | July 2022 | 33,472 | 41.1% | December 2025 |
| Third revision | December 2025 | 32,718 | 38.3% | December 2028 |

The cost rose 48.8%. The government's own contribution rose 132%, from Tk 5,390 crore to Tk 12,522 crore, while the loan-financed part rose 22% in taka terms. Cost per route-kilometre went from Tk 1,094 crore to Tk 1,539 crore.

## 3. Why did cost and time escalate?

- **Scope added late.** The 1.16 km Kamalapur extension, extra land (27 hectares to 45 hectares provided for) and station plazas were added in 2022, ten years into the project.
- **Currency.** Taka depreciation raised the taka value of loan-funded items.
- **Schedule.** JICA's 2013 appraisal expected the full line by December 2021; all 16 Uttara–Motijheel stations opened in December 2023. The Kamalapur extension was planned for June 2025 and is now targeted for July 2027, at about 85% complete.
- **Later corrections.** The third revision cut Tk 1,212 crore of land and dropped four station plazas, while adding rolling stock, consultancy and loan-repayment costs.

Spending discipline was otherwise reasonable: 87% of allocations were used over the project's life. Component-level cost tables were not found, so the escalation cannot be fully itemised.

## 4. How many people use the metro?

Reported daily ridership rose from 3,857 on the first day to about 70,000 in mid-2023, 275,000–290,000 in March 2024, and above 450,000 by September 2026. The record is **499,756 on 10 September 2026**. Cumulative ridership reached **313.5 million** by August 2026. Motijheel is the busiest station. The line carries about 1% of Dhaka's daily trips.

## 5. Is ridership growth sustainable?

Ridership has reached the 500,000-a-day design figure for Uttara–Motijheel, so growth now depends on capacity and network. In September 2025 only 13 of 24 trains were in constant use; more were deployed after October 2025. In the simulation, 1.7% of trains reach capacity on their busiest section, almost all southbound between 08:00 and 10:00. DMTCL projects 677,000 a day after Kamalapur opens; that is used here only as the high case. A fatal bearing-pad failure in October 2025 was followed by a reported 10% dip in ridership, which shows that confidence in safety is part of sustainability.

## 6. Does ticket revenue cover operating expenses?

Audited ticket sales were Tk 18 crore in FY2022-23, Tk 246 crore in FY2023-24 and **Tk 418 crore in FY2024-25**. Non-fare income is about 4% of that. The true operating cost is not public: DMTCL's accounts exclude electricity, most maintenance and loan service, which are still paid from the project budget. DMTCL does not yet recognise ticket revenue as income, because the assets have not been transferred to it, and it reports negative equity.

On simulated costs, revenue of about Tk 551 crore covered operating cost of about Tk 300 crore in FY2025-26. That margin narrows from 2026 as DMTCL takes over maintenance. Fares have not changed since 2022, so the real fare is falling with inflation.

## 7. How much commuting time is saved?

Reported evidence: Uttara to Motijheel took 105 minutes by road in 2009 and about two hours recently, against 36–40 minutes by metro. Modelled for the average 7.7 km trip, a passenger saves about **20 minutes door to door**, and a former bus passenger about 29 minutes. That is roughly **45 million passenger-hours in FY2025-26**, valued at about Tk 398 crore. Surveyed users rate time savings highest of all attributes (4.39 out of 5).

## 8. Has it reduced road transport demand?

Partly, and perhaps temporarily. A JICA research paper using Google Maps data found road speeds near the line rose 10–15% relative to comparison corridors up to June 2024, with signs that the gain faded as new traffic filled the space. Surveys show nearly 60% of metro passengers came from buses and only about 12% from private vehicles. The metro has mainly replaced bus travel, not car travel.

## 9. What are the environmental benefits?

No measured air-quality or energy data were found. The model suggests the line currently **emits about 34,000 tonnes more CO2 a year than it avoids**, because most riders came from full buses and the grid is carbon-intensive. This contradicts the pre-opening claim of more than 200,000 tonnes saved. It depends on modelled electricity use and on how many buses were actually withdrawn, and it would reverse with a cleaner grid or a larger shift from private vehicles. Local benefits are clearer: about 11 million litres of fuel and 6 tonnes of fine particles avoided a year.

## 10. What economic activity has developed around stations?

Press and property-portal reports describe rents in Mirpur up 27% and flat prices in Uttara and Mirpur up about 25% in a year, with developers also citing construction costs and planning rules. No station-level transaction, business or employment data exists publicly, so the metro's share of these changes cannot be measured. RAJUK has allocated 32 acres at Uttara for transit-oriented development. The station-area dataset in this package is a simulation built on an assumed premium, not evidence.

## 11. What are the major risks?

- **Debt service.** Estimated repayments rise from about Tk 460 crore in FY2026-27 to over Tk 1,000 crore a year from FY2030-31, far above any plausible operating surplus.
- **Fares.** A frozen fare erodes real revenue each year; a rise tests affordability, which users already rate low.
- **Costs moving to DMTCL.** Maintenance and equipment costs transfer from the project; officials expected annual expenses to more than double.
- **Structural safety.** Two bearing-pad failures, one fatal.
- **Capacity.** Peak trains are full; staffing limited frequency for years.
- **Governance and transparency.** Assets not transferred, share capital largely unpaid, no published operating statistics.
- **Access.** Feeder services and walking conditions are the weakest-rated part of the journey.

## 12. What are the long-term economic returns?

| Case | Economic return | Benefit-cost ratio at 12% |
|---|---|---|
| Base case | −2.3% | 0.13 |
| High demand | −1.8% | 0.15 |
| Combined downside | −4.3% | 0.09 |
| Most favourable tested | 2.9% | 0.34 |
| JICA appraisals (2013–2020) | 25.4%–27.9% | — |

Benefits would need to be about nine times larger to reach 12%. The whole-project financial return is about −3.4%, against JICA's 1.7–3.1%.

This model is conservative by construction. It counts only time, vehicle-cost and emission benefits to metro passengers through FY2044-45 and leaves out road users, safety, health, land value and wider economic effects. JICA's workings were not available. Even so, three differences are measurable: passenger-kilometres are about 2.8 million a day against appraisal targets of 4.2 million and later 8.6 million; trips average 7.7 km on a 20 km line; and the cost is 49% higher.

## 13. Lessons before approving another metro

1. **Fix the scope before approval.** The largest cost and time overruns came from adding an extension and land a decade in.
2. **Test appraisals against Line-6 outcomes.** Use observed trip lengths, mode shift and road-speed effects, and publish the workings.
3. **Publish operating data.** Monthly ridership, revenue, energy and punctuality should be public; their absence is why much of this analysis had to be simulated.
4. **Settle the operator's finances first.** Decide who services the debt, transfer the assets, and set a fare policy that tracks costs.
5. **Plan the network, feeders and land together.** A single line that mainly replaces buses delivers limited decongestion and climate benefit.
6. **Budget for maintenance and structural inspection from day one.**
7. **Manage currency risk on foreign loans explicitly.**

## Recommendation

- **Operational and social viability: established.** The line is heavily used, valued by passengers and should be run at full capacity, with more trains in the peak and better station access.
- **Financial viability: not established.** The line can plausibly fund its operations but not its debt. The government should state this plainly, fund debt service from the budget, and adopt a transparent fare and subsidy policy.
- **Economic viability: unproven on public evidence.** Returns on direct passenger benefits are low. Future lines should be approved only after an independent appraisal that uses Line-6's actual results, includes realistic network effects, and passes at conservative assumptions.

The priority is not whether to build more urban rail, but in what order and on what evidence: complete and connect lines that lengthen trips and feed Line-6, and make DMTCL's operating data public so the next assessment can rest on measurement instead of simulation.
